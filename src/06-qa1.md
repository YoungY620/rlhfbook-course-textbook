## Course orientation

> **What prerequisites should a learner cover before this course?**

The course is designed for early AI PhD or MS students. The traditional path is:

- Basics of language modeling and NLP (what a tokenizer, transformer, and language-model loss are).
- Basic machine learning (an introductory AI course and an introductory ML course).

With the rise of AI agents, much of the material is also accessible to motivated practitioners. The lectures can be watched out of order, and it is fine to chase rabbit holes. For classical RL background, useful references are David Silver's RL Course (2015), UC Berkeley Deep RL (2023), and Sutton & Barto (2018).

## Data & instruction tuning

> **For synthetic SFT data, is one teacher model better than many?**

Empirically, recent open recipes often get the best results from a *single* strong teacher. OpenThoughts3 used QwQ-32B as the sole teacher to generate 1.2M reasoning examples (Guha et al. 2025). Olmo 3's reasoning SFT stage followed a similar pattern, using one strong teacher to produce a large synthetic mix (Team Olmo 2025). Qwen3-32B and QwQ-32B-era teachers still look unusually strong in practice. The open question is why bigger, newer, or "smarter" models do not always transfer better — distribution match, verbosity, formatting, and over-editing may all play a role.

> **Should the teacher model's distribution match the base model?**

In practice, yes, but the effect is not fully understood. A teacher that is close to the base model in style, vocabulary, and reasoning patterns tends to produce completions that are easier for the student to imitate. A much larger or more capable teacher may generate answers the base model cannot reliably reproduce, which wastes gradient budget or introduces hallucinated behavior. There is no clean theoretical rule; it is an active empirical question.

> **How important is balancing domains in an SFT mix?**

Very important for building a general model, and the practical solution is not surprising: harder tasks usually get more tokens. A good mix needs enough representation of each downstream domain (chat, math, code, instruction following, safety, etc.) and enough weight on the hard ones so the model does not underfit them. The art is in the mix and the curation loop: combine existing datasets, evaluate, identify weaknesses, and add targeted data. Scaling one domain without checking others tends to regress general capability.

## Reward models & reward design

> **Is there a unified benchmark across pairwise RMs, ORMs, PRMs, and generative RMs?**

No. The four model classes need different supervision and produce different outputs:

| Model | Input | Output | Supervision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pairwise RM | prompt + two completions | scalar preference score | chosen/rejected pairs |
| ORM | prompt + one completion | per-token correctness probability | binary outcome label |
| PRM | prompt + reasoning trace | score at step boundaries | step-level labels |
| Generative RM | prompt + two completions | natural-language verdict | prompt-based judging |

A single labeled dataset could in principle be re-annotated for all four, but the community has fragmented into separate benchmarks (RewardBench, RewardBench2, PRM Bench, ProcessBench, RewardMATH, etc.). This fragmentation is normal for a maturing field, but it makes apples-to-apples comparisons difficult. The first reward-model benchmark, RewardBench, helped establish common infrastructure (Lambert et al. 2025), and the landscape has since expanded rapidly.

> **Why choose token-level rewards over sequence-level rewards?**

The phrase has two meanings, and the answer depends on which one is meant.

**Reward granularity** refers to *what* gets a reward: only the final answer (sequence-level) versus intermediate signals such as step correctness, KL penalties, or format rewards (token-level or step-level). Process reward models (PRMs) were introduced precisely to provide denser, step-level supervision for reasoning (Lightman et al. 2024). Denser rewards can help credit assignment, especially in long chain-of-thought training.

**Loss granularity** refers to *how* per-token losses are averaged. Two common patterns are:

```python
# Sequence-normalized: each completion has equal weight
loss = ((pg_loss * mask).sum(dim=1) / mask.sum(dim=1)).mean()

# Token-normalized: each generated token has equal weight
loss = (pg_loss * mask).sum() / mask.sum()
```

Token-level normalization is used by DAPO (Yu et al. 2025). It gives longer completions proportionally more gradient, which can help long-CoT RL because the tokens that were actually sampled receive more learning signal. The counterpoint is that if the reward is only final-answer correctness, a sequence-level advantage is still natural; GSPO pushes the importance ratio back to the sequence level (Zheng et al. 2025).

## RL math & notation

> **Is GAE the only way to compute the advantage in PPO?**

No. The PPO clipped objective from the RL cheatsheet is:

$$
J(\\theta) = \\mathbb{E}\_t\\!\\left[\\min\\!\\left(\\rho\_t(\\theta)\\hat{A}\_t,\\; \\text{clip}(\\rho\_t(\\theta),\\, 1-\\varepsilon,\\, 1+\\varepsilon)\\hat{A}\_t\\right)\\right]
$$

where $\\hat{A}\_t$ can be computed in many ways. Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) is the most common choice (Schulman et al. 2015), but it is not required. Alternatives include:

- Monte Carlo: $\\hat{A}\_t = G\_t - V(s\_t)$, full return minus the value baseline.
- One-step TD: $\\hat{A}\_t = r\_t + \\gamma V(s\_{t+1}) - V(s\_t)$.
- n-step returns.

In practice, almost all PPO implementations for LLMs use GAE because it balances bias and variance well, with $\\lambda \\approx 0.95$ and $\\gamma = 1.0$. A raw value model without GAE is theoretically possible but uncommon.

> **What is the shape of $\\rho$?**

The importance-sampling ratio is:

$$
\\rho\_t(\\theta) = \\frac{\\pi\_\\theta(a\_t \\mid s\_t)}{\\pi\_{\\theta\_\\text{old}}(a\_t \\mid s\_t)}.
$$

In language-model code the shapes are:

- $\\pi(\\cdot \\mid s\_t)$ is vocab-shaped: `[B, T, V]`.
- $\\pi(a\_t \\mid s\_t)$ is gathered at the sampled tokens: `[B, T]`.
- $\\rho\_t$ is scalar per token; the rollout tensor is `[B, T]`.

```python
# logits/logprobs over vocab: [B, T, V]
old_logps_all = old_policy.log_softmax(logits_old, dim=-1)
new_logps_all = policy.log_softmax(logits_new, dim=-1)

# sampled token ids: [B, T]
actions = input_ids[:, 1:]

# gather only the probabilities of the sampled tokens: [B, T]
old_logps = old_logps_all.gather(-1, actions.unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)
new_logps = new_logps_all.gather(-1, actions.unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)

# importance ratio per sampled token: [B, T]
rho = torch.exp(new_logps - old_logps)
```

## PPO implementation & debugging

> **Why does PPO use `torch.max` for both policy and value losses?**

Because PPO is written as a **loss to minimize**, the `torch.max` selects the more conservative of the clipped and unclipped terms. The policy loss is:

```python
# PPO minimizes a negative policy loss
pg_loss_unclipped = -advantages * ratio
pg_loss_clipped = -advantages * torch.clamp(
    ratio, 1.0 - eps, 1.0 + eps
)

# choose the more conservative / worse loss
pg_loss = torch.max(pg_loss_unclipped, pg_loss_clipped)
```

When the advantage is positive, the unclipped term wants to increase the action's probability. Once the ratio has moved past $1 + \\varepsilon$, the clipped term becomes larger (less negative), so `torch.max` picks it and the gradient stops. This prevents over-reinforcing. When the advantage is negative, the symmetric logic prevents over-suppressing.

The value function uses the same pattern:

```python
# value clipping: do not let critic jump too far
v_clip = old_values + torch.clamp(
    v_pred - old_values, -vf_eps, vf_eps
)

vf_unclipped = 0.5 * (v_pred - targets) ** 2
vf_clipped = 0.5 * (v_clip - targets) ** 2

# optimize the worse value error
vf_loss = torch.max(vf_unclipped, vf_clipped)
```

Policy clipping limits policy drift; value clipping limits critic drift. In both cases `torch.max` trains on the worse error, which keeps updates conservative.

## Human feedback operations

> **How should data-annotation companies stay valuable as synthetic data gets simpler?**

The value is shifting from low-skill, high-volume labeling to expert-domain work. As frontier models generate high-quality synthetic answers for routine questions, human annotators are increasingly needed in specialized areas such as law, finance, healthcare, and consulting, where correctness, nuance, and trust matter. The business model moves from "more labelers" to "better experts" and to tooling that lets those experts give richer, more reliable feedback.

## Key Takeaways

- **Prerequisites:** language modeling basics plus introductory ML are enough to start; the course is aimed at early graduate students but is also accessible to practitioners.
- **Synthetic SFT:** one strong, well-matched teacher is often better than many; domain balance and distribution match matter more than raw teacher size.
- **Reward-model landscape:** pairwise RMs, ORMs, PRMs, and generative RMs serve different roles and are evaluated on different benchmarks; no unified benchmark exists yet.
- **Token-level vs sequence-level:** distinguish reward granularity (final answer vs step/token rewards) from loss granularity (per-sequence vs per-token averaging). Each choice changes length bias and credit assignment.
- **PPO advantages:** GAE is standard but not mandatory; $\\rho\_t$ is a per-token scalar of shape `[B, T]` computed from log-probability differences.
- **PPO clipping:** `torch.max` on the negative loss picks the conservative branch, preventing over-reinforcement and over-suppression. The value function uses the same idea.
- **Human feedback operations:** annotation value is moving up the skill ladder into expert domains, not disappearing.
